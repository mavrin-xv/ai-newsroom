# How to close your books in 5 working days instead of 9

One pilot team spent 9 working days on their month-end close. The owner saw real numbers on day 10, a third of the way into the next month. Across the 14 firms in our [pilot](https://example.org/pilot-report), the median close went from 9 working days to 5 after four changes. None of them needs new software.

## Summary

- Photograph a receipt the day the money is spent; it removes most of the chasing.
- Match the bank every week, so month-end holds one week of lines, not four.
- Run one checklist in the same order every month; payroll and accruals come after the bank match.
- Send the accountant's list on day 2, with open questions, not on day 6 with everything done.

## Step 1: Stop chasing receipts at the end

The shoebox is where month-end time goes. In our pilot, 31% of expense lines had no receipt on the first day of close ([pilot report](https://example.org/pilot-report), 14 firms, April 2026). Every one of those is a message to a colleague, a wait, and a reminder.

Set one rule: a receipt is photographed the day the money is spent, or the card is paused. It sounds harsh. It works because the cost lands on the person who can fix it.

Here is the part nobody tells you: the rule only holds if someone checks it weekly. Put the receipt count on the same Tuesday slot as the bank match in Step 2.

## Step 2: Match the bank weekly, not monthly

Doing the bank match once a month means four weeks of lines at once, and the odd ones are a month old by then. Nobody remembers what a payment from five weeks ago was for.

Pick a fixed slot, Tuesday morning for example, and match everything that cleared since the last slot. Anything you cannot match goes on a short list with a name next to it. The person named answers before the next slot.

For a firm with a few hundred lines a month, the weekly slot is short. Time yours for the first month and keep the number. When someone asks in month three why the team still does this on Tuesdays, that number answers them.

Two lines will keep coming back: card refunds that land weeks after the purchase, and transfers between your own accounts. Give each a fixed rule the first time you see it, write the rule next to the checklist, and stop deciding them again every week.

## Step 3: Use one checklist, in the same order, every month

```text
[ ] all receipts in
[ ] bank matched to the last day of the month
[ ] payroll journal posted
[ ] accruals for unbilled work
[ ] accountant's list sent
```

The order matters. Payroll and accruals depend on a clean bank match, so doing them first means doing them twice. Print the list, tick it on paper, and write the day each line was done. After two months you will see which step eats the time.

## Step 4: Send the accountant's list on day 2

Your accountant cannot start until they have your questions. Send the list on day 2 with what you know and what you do not, instead of on day 6 with everything finished.

A good list has three parts: lines you could not match, entries you are unsure how to book, and anything unusual this month (a new hire, a large one-off purchase, a refund). Keep it to one page.

Ask the accountant to answer in the same document, line by line. By month three the list gets shorter, because the answers from earlier months become rules your team already follows, and day 2 stops feeling early.

## When this does not apply

- Under a few dozen bank lines a month, a weekly match is overhead. Match monthly and keep the checklist.
- The card-pause rule does not cover cash or out-of-pocket spend. For those, set a cut-off day for expense claims instead.
- If your accountant closes the books for you, Steps 1 and 4 still help; Steps 2 and 3 are theirs.
- If the close is slow because the chart of accounts is a mess, fix that first. No routine survives a ledger nobody trusts.
